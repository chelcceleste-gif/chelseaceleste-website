Hormonal Acne and Diet: How to Clear Your Skin From the Inside Out

The breakouts on your chin and jaw aren't random — they're a message from your hormones. Here's exactly what the science says about diet, androgens, and clearing your skin for good.

C
Chelsea Celeste MSc Nutrition & Human Performance · @chelcceleste

I want to start with something that took me years to fully understand: hormonal acne is not a skincare problem. It's a hormonal problem — and hormones, in large part, are driven by what you eat. No cleanser, toner, or spot treatment will ever fully address a breakout that started in your bloodstream. That's not to say topical products don't play a role, but if you've been patching over hormonal acne with products and feeling frustrated that it keeps coming back, this post is for you.

As an MSc nutritionist who has worked with countless women navigating persistent breakouts, I've seen the same pattern again and again. The breakouts cluster along the chin, jawline, and lower cheeks. They show up in the week before a period. They feel deep and cystic rather than small and surface-level. They laugh in the face of salicylic acid. Sound familiar? This is the fingerprint of hormonally-driven acne, and it responds beautifully — sometimes dramatically — to targeted changes in diet.

In this post, I'm going to walk you through the precise biological mechanisms linking food to hormonal acne, the key foods to prioritise, and the ones to limit or remove. I'll also share the nutrients that act like natural hormonal regulators and give you a clear picture of what a "skin-supportive" day of eating actually looks like. Let's go deep.

What Is Hormonal Acne, Really?

Hormonal acne is driven primarily by androgens — the group of hormones that includes testosterone and its more potent derivative, dihydrotestosterone (DHT). Androgens stimulate sebaceous glands to produce more sebum (skin oil). When sebum production is elevated, the pore environment becomes more favourable for the acne-causing bacteria Cutibacterium acnes, leading to the clogged pores, inflammation, and lesions we call acne.

Here's the crucial point most people miss: you don't need abnormally high testosterone levels to have hormonal acne. What matters is how sensitive your skin cells' androgen receptors are, and how much free testosterone is circulating in your blood. Both of these are significantly influenced by diet — particularly by insulin levels, the quality of your fats, and specific micronutrients.

The other hormone in the hormonal acne story is IGF-1 (insulin-like growth factor 1), a growth signalling molecule that behaves very similarly to insulin in its effect on the skin. IGF-1 directly stimulates sebum production and increases androgen activity in the skin, making it one of the most important — and most dietary-responsive — drivers of acne.

The Insulin–Androgen Connection

This is the most important mechanism to understand if you want to clear hormonal acne through diet. When you eat foods that cause a rapid spike in blood sugar — refined carbohydrates, sugary drinks, white bread, pastries — your pancreas releases insulin to bring that sugar back down. So far, so normal. But chronically elevated insulin, or even repeated large insulin spikes throughout the day, has a cascade of effects on your hormones that are genuinely terrible for your skin.

High insulin does three things that promote hormonal acne. First, it stimulates the ovaries (in women) and adrenal glands to produce more androgens. Second, it suppresses the liver's production of sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG), a protein that binds testosterone and keeps it inactive in the blood. Less SHBG means more free testosterone circulating and available to activate sebaceous glands. Third, insulin activates a cellular growth pathway called mTORC1 that promotes sebocyte (oil gland cell) proliferation and sebum synthesis.

The research here is compelling. A 2007 randomised controlled trial published in the American Journal of Clinical Nutrition found that young men on a low-glycaemic diet for 12 weeks had significantly fewer acne lesions, lower free androgen levels, and improved insulin sensitivity compared to those eating a typical Western diet. Multiple studies since have replicated and expanded these findings. Low-glycaemic eating is now one of the most evidence-supported dietary interventions for acne in the scientific literature.

High-GI Foods Most Likely to Drive Hormonal Acne
  • White bread, bagels, white rice, and white pasta
  • Sugary breakfast cereals and instant oats
  • Fruit juice, soft drinks, energy drinks, and sweetened coffees
  • Pastries, cakes, biscuits, and confectionery
  • Highly processed snack foods (crackers, rice cakes, crisps)
  • Alcohol, particularly beer and sweet cocktails

Swapping these for their low-GI counterparts — oats over cornflakes, legumes over white rice, whole fruit over juice — keeps insulin in a steadier, lower range throughout the day. Over time, this reduces free androgen levels, increases SHBG, and dials down the mTORC1 signalling that drives oil production. The skin gets noticeably less congested within four to eight weeks for most people I work with.

Dairy, IGF-1, and Your Skin

Dairy is the most discussed dietary trigger for acne, and the science behind it is more nuanced than "dairy causes breakouts in everyone." The mechanism primarily runs through IGF-1. Cow's milk naturally contains IGF-1 (dairy cows are pregnant for most of their milking lives, making them hormonally active), and milk consumption also triggers the body to produce more of its own IGF-1. This dual elevation of circulating IGF-1 has been shown in multiple studies to directly stimulate sebum production and promote acne pathogenesis.

Interestingly, it's not just the fat content of dairy that matters — skim milk has been shown in some studies to have a stronger association with acne than full-fat milk, possibly because the fat normally present in whole milk buffers the glycaemic response and because skim milk undergoes processing that concentrates its hormonal precursors. Whey protein, which is a dairy derivative and a staple in many fitness communities, is a particularly potent stimulator of IGF-1 and has a strong association with acne in both case studies and observational data.

The practical takeaway isn't necessarily to eliminate all dairy forever. Rather, I suggest a structured six-week elimination trial of all cow's milk dairy — including milk, yoghurt, cheese, butter, and whey protein — to assess your individual response. Many of my clients see dramatic reductions in breakout frequency and severity within the first three to four weeks. If reintroduction triggers breakouts, you have your answer. If not, you can likely tolerate dairy in moderate amounts.

If you're a regular whey protein user, consider switching to a pea protein or hemp protein supplement for the duration of the trial. This single swap has been transformative for several of my clients who were otherwise eating extremely well and couldn't understand why their skin wasn't clearing.

The Foods That Lower Androgens Naturally

This is the section I love most, because it moves us from restriction to abundance. There is a genuinely impressive body of research on foods and nutrients that naturally modulate androgen activity — reducing testosterone conversion, increasing SHBG, or reducing androgen receptor sensitivity. Here are the most evidence-backed.

Flaxseed

Flaxseeds are the richest dietary source of lignans, a type of phytoestrogen that has demonstrated clear anti-androgenic effects in both animal and human studies. Lignans compete with androgens at receptor sites and have been shown to increase SHBG levels, effectively reducing the amount of free testosterone available to act on sebaceous glands. One tablespoon of ground flaxseed daily is a meaningful dose — add it to smoothies, yoghurt alternatives, porridge, or salad dressings. Ground flaxseed is far better absorbed than whole seeds, which can pass through the gut largely intact.

Spearmint Tea

This is one of the most exciting findings in nutritional dermatology. Two well-designed studies — a pilot study and a subsequent randomised controlled trial — found that drinking two cups of spearmint tea daily significantly reduced free testosterone levels in women with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), a condition characterised by hyperandrogenism and very commonly associated with hormonal acne. The effect was measurable within 30 days and continued to improve over five weeks. Spearmint contains compounds that appear to inhibit the production of androgens in the adrenal glands and ovaries. It's gentle, delicious, and available anywhere. Two cups a day is a small habit with potentially significant payoff.

Green Tea (EGCG)

Green tea contains epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), a catechin polyphenol with a remarkably well-documented anti-acne profile. EGCG has been shown to inhibit sebocyte lipid production, reduce androgen receptor activity, suppress the mTORC1 pathway (the same one activated by high insulin), and provide potent anti-inflammatory effects in skin tissue. In a 2016 randomised controlled trial, a green tea extract supplement reduced total sebum production by approximately 70% in participants over eight weeks. Two to three cups of quality green tea daily provides a meaningful dose of EGCG, or you can consider a standardised extract supplement.

Omega-3 Fatty Acids and Inflammation

Acne is fundamentally an inflammatory condition. While androgens and insulin drive sebum overproduction and clogged pores, it's inflammation that turns a blocked pore into an angry, red, painful lesion. Omega-3 fatty acids — particularly EPA (eicosapentaenoic acid) and DHA (docosahexaenoic acid), found in fatty fish, algae oil, and quality supplements — are among the most potent dietary anti-inflammatory agents we have access to.

Omega-3s work by competing with omega-6 fatty acids for the same metabolic enzymes. Omega-6 fatty acids (abundant in seed oils, processed foods, and conventional meat) are precursors to pro-inflammatory eicosanoids. When you shift the dietary ratio toward omega-3, you reduce the production of these pro-inflammatory compounds and increase the production of anti-inflammatory resolvins and protectins. In the context of acne, this means less inflammatory activation of the follicle and less severe lesions even when sebum is elevated.

A 2012 study published in Lipids in Health and Disease found that participants supplementing with omega-3 fatty acids for 10 weeks saw significant reductions in both inflammatory and non-inflammatory acne lesions. Aim for two to three servings of fatty fish per week (salmon, sardines, mackerel, anchovies), or supplement with 2–3g combined EPA+DHA daily from a quality fish oil or algae-based alternative. This is one of the core recommendations in my Eat Your Skincare bundle, alongside the full food guide and meal plan for skin-supportive eating.

Equally important is reducing your omega-6 intake. The typical Western diet has an omega-6 to omega-3 ratio of around 15:1, when our evolutionary optimal is closer to 4:1. Cutting back on vegetable and seed oils (sunflower, canola, soybean, corn oil) and processed foods dramatically shifts this ratio in your favour.

Zinc: The Skin's Most Important Mineral

Zinc deserves its own section because the evidence for its role in acne is outstanding. Zinc is required for over 300 enzymatic reactions in the body, including several directly relevant to acne: it inhibits 5-alpha-reductase (the enzyme that converts testosterone into the more potent DHT), reduces sebum production, supports skin cell turnover, and exerts direct antimicrobial effects against C. acnes. Multiple meta-analyses have confirmed that people with acne tend to have significantly lower serum zinc levels than those without, and that zinc supplementation meaningfully reduces acne severity.

Zinc gluconate and zinc glycinate are the best-absorbed supplemental forms. Typical therapeutic doses used in studies range from 30–45mg elemental zinc daily, though it's always best to work with a practitioner when supplementing at therapeutic levels since zinc competes with copper for absorption. From a food perspective, the richest sources of zinc are oysters (extraordinarily dense — one oyster can contain over 70% of your daily requirement), red meat, pumpkin seeds, hemp seeds, chickpeas, and lentils. Note that zinc from plant sources is less bioavailable due to phytate content — soaking and sprouting legumes and seeds improves absorption significantly.

Magnesium, Blood Sugar, and Hormonal Balance

Magnesium is one of the most commonly deficient minerals in the modern diet, and its relationship to hormonal acne is significant. Magnesium plays a central role in insulin sensitivity — a well-magnesium-nourished body manages blood sugar more efficiently, which directly reduces the insulin spikes that drive androgen overproduction. Studies show that magnesium deficiency is associated with impaired insulin signalling and elevated fasting glucose, both of which worsen hormonal acne.

Magnesium also supports the adrenal glands, which are a major site of androgen production, particularly of DHEA (dehydroepiandrosterone), a precursor to testosterone. Chronic stress depletes magnesium and drives adrenal androgen output simultaneously — which is one reason stress so reliably triggers breakouts. Replenishing magnesium helps blunt both the stress response and the downstream hormonal consequences for skin.

Best dietary sources include dark leafy greens (spinach, chard), pumpkin seeds, black beans, almonds, and dark chocolate (70%+). Magnesium glycinate is an excellent supplement form — 200–400mg before bed supports both magnesium repletion and sleep quality, which is its own important variable for hormonal skin health.

Antioxidants, Vitamin A, and the Sebaceous Gland

Vitamin A is the nutrient most directly linked to sebum regulation. It works by binding to retinoic acid receptors in sebocytes, reducing their proliferation and their rate of sebum synthesis. This is precisely the mechanism exploited by prescription retinoids like isotretinoin (Accutane) — which is essentially a synthetic, pharmaceutical-strength form of retinoic acid. While you won't get Accutane-level effects from food alone, adequate dietary vitamin A is genuinely important for keeping sebaceous activity in a healthy range.

Preformed vitamin A is found in liver, egg yolks, and dairy. Provitamin A carotenoids (which the body converts to retinol) are found in orange, yellow, and dark green vegetables — sweet potato, carrots, butternut squash, spinach, and kale are particularly rich sources. Selenium, another skin-critical mineral found in Brazil nuts (just two to three per day meets your daily need), works synergistically with vitamin A and has antioxidant activity that reduces the oxidative stress involved in acne lesion formation.

What a Skin-Supportive Day of Eating Looks Like

Putting this all together doesn't need to be complicated. Here's a practical framework for a day of eating that actively works to balance hormones, reduce inflammation, and support skin clarity:

Breakfast: Overnight oats (rolled oats, not instant) with ground flaxseed, mixed berries, and a tablespoon of almond butter — slow-release carbohydrates, lignans, antioxidants, and healthy fats, with no blood sugar spike.

Mid-morning: Two cups of spearmint tea with a small handful of pumpkin seeds — anti-androgenic, zinc-rich, satisfying.

Lunch: A large salad with tinned sardines or wild salmon, avocado, cucumber, mixed leaves, and a dressing of olive oil and lemon — omega-3s, vitamin E, and monounsaturated fats that support skin barrier function alongside anti-inflammatory fatty acids.

Afternoon: Green tea and two Brazil nuts — EGCG, selenium, and a gentle caffeine lift without a sugar crash.

Dinner: Lentil and sweet potato curry with wilted spinach and brown rice — zinc, magnesium, vitamin A, folate, and fibre to support oestrogen clearance and blood sugar stability overnight.

This isn't a rigid prescription — it's an illustration of how easily the key skin-supportive nutrients can slot into everyday eating without restriction or complexity. What to Avoid: A Clear Summary

While I always prefer to approach nutrition from an abundance mindset — adding in the good rather than just removing things — it's worth being clear about the dietary inputs most likely to perpetuate hormonal acne:

  • High-GI refined carbohydrates — white bread, white rice, pastries, sugary cereals, sweets, and fruit juice spike insulin and drive androgen production.
  • Cow's milk dairy — particularly skim milk and whey protein — elevates IGF-1 and stimulates sebum production. A trial elimination is worth doing.
  • Seed and vegetable oils high in omega-6 — sunflower, corn, soybean, and canola oils tip the omega-6:omega-3 ratio toward inflammation.
  • Alcohol — disrupts liver function (which clears excess oestrogen and androgens), raises blood sugar, and impairs sleep, all of which worsen hormonal skin.
  • Ultra-processed foods — combine high GI, omega-6 oils, and additives that disrupt the gut microbiome, which has its own connection to skin health via the gut-skin axis.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take to see results when you change your diet for hormonal acne?

Most people begin to notice changes in their skin within four to six weeks of consistent dietary changes. The first thing that tends to improve is the inflammatory severity of breakouts — new spots become smaller and less angry. A significant reduction in breakout frequency typically takes eight to twelve weeks, which aligns with the skin cell turnover cycle. Hormonal changes, like increased SHBG or reduced free testosterone, can take a full three-month cycle to fully register. Consistency is everything — short-term dietary changes produce short-term results.

Is hormonal acne different in PCOS versus a typical hormonal imbalance?

The underlying mechanism is similar — elevated androgens and often elevated insulin — but PCOS typically involves a more significant degree of both hyperandrogenism and insulin resistance, which can make the dietary intervention both more impactful and more necessary. Women with PCOS often see remarkable skin improvements from low-GI eating, spearmint tea, inositol (a B-vitamin-like compound), and zinc. If you suspect PCOS, I strongly recommend getting a proper diagnosis and working with both a GP and a nutritionist who specialises in hormonal health.

Do I need to cut out sugar completely to clear hormonal acne?

No, and I want to be clear about this because "cut out all sugar" is both unsustainable and unnecessary. The goal is to stabilise insulin levels and reduce the frequency and magnitude of blood sugar spikes — not to achieve a zero-sugar diet. Whole fruit, for example, contains fructose but also fibre, water, and polyphenols that buffer the glycaemic response significantly. Honey and maple syrup in small quantities in an otherwise balanced meal have minimal hormonal impact. The biggest wins come from reducing the obvious high-volume sugar sources: sugary drinks, confectionery, and refined baked goods.

Can supplements alone clear hormonal acne without changing my diet?

Supplements can move the needle meaningfully — zinc, omega-3, and magnesium in particular — but they work best as amplifiers of good dietary foundations, not replacements for them. If your diet is high in refined carbohydrates and omega-6 oils, a zinc supplement will provide some benefit but will be working against significant headwinds. The most powerful results I see are when dietary changes and targeted supplementation work together. That said, if changing everything at once feels overwhelming, starting with supplements while you gradually improve your diet is a completely valid approach.
